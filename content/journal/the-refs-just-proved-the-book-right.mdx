Caitlin Clark took a shot to the throat from Alyssa Thomas during a loose-ball sequence. Officials reviewed it live. They declined a flagrant.

Days later, after the clip had already circled the league, the WNBA quietly upgraded the call to a Flagrant 2 and suspended Thomas one game. Not in the moment, when it mattered for the players on the floor. After.

That gap — between what the refs call live and what the league admits later — is the whole book.

This week alone, a WNBA head coach called a no-call "a f------ joke" to reporters. Sophie Cunningham got whistled on a play where she took the contact, not gave it. Coaches and GMs from eight different teams told Yahoo Sports the officiating task force Commissioner Cathy Engelbert promised at last year's All-Star Weekend still hasn't solved the consistency problem it was built to solve. And Clark herself, usually careful with what she says publicly, came out and said the league needs to "pay them like they're full-time employees" — because the refs aren't the ones failing, the system around them is.

None of this is new. It's the same pattern documented across multiple seasons in *UNWHISTLED: How the WNBA Failed Caitlin Clark* — inconsistent enforcement, delayed accountability, and a league that reviews the tape only after the backlash forces it to. The task force exists. The upgrade happened. The suspension came down. All of it, days too late, after the damage was already done on the floor and in the discourse.

Question the call. Protect the game. That's not a slogan — it's what happens when nobody else will.
